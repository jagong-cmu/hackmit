Can you actually build an elder-care AI assistant on Meta Ray-Ban glasses?

Short answer: yes for two of the five features, "yes with real limits" for two more, and one is legally live-wire territory that Meta itself just backed away from. Below is the feature-by-feature breakdown, the actual developer-access picture as of September 2026, and a build order for the weekend.

The platform reality: glasses are a peripheral, not a computer

Meta opened a real, official SDK in September 2025 — the Wearables Device Access Toolkit (DAT), now at v0.6 (Swift/Kotlin, Android + iOS). It is a phone-side SDK: your code runs on the paired phone, and the glasses are a Bluetooth peripheral exposing camera, mic array, and speaker. Nothing you write runs on the glasses' own chip. That's actually good news for a hackathon: it's a normal mobile app with a fancy peripheral, not embedded firmware work.

Status caveat: DAT is in public developer preview. Fine for sideloaded hackathon builds on your own hardware — not yet cleared for public app-store distribution (Meta's own GA timeline for that is unclear/contested across sources). Build and demo freely; don't plan a launch on it yet.

Camera — real, works

12MP ultra-wide: still photos with EXIF, and live streaming up to 720p/30fps to the phone. Proven end-to-end by the 2024 Harvard "I-XRAY" project.

Mic — real, but not the hotword

5-mic array is streamable. "Hey Meta" itself is fixed and confirmed non-extensible by Meta's own dev forum — you run your own keyword spotter on top.

IMU / calling — unconfirmed

Accelerometer/IMU access is described as "eventually" rolling out. No evidence DAT can place a phone call at all — that stays a normal phone-app job.

Talking back to the wearer: confirmed — audio-only, Gen 2

Hardware confirmed: Ray-Ban Meta Gen 2. This settles the open question — camera + 5-mic array + open-ear speakers, no display, no Neural Band. Every feature below is audio-only by hardware, not by choice. That's not a downgrade from the plan: audio-first was already the top recommendation, so this just removes the "maybe add a display card" stretch idea entirely. Design for voice in and voice out, full stop.
What Gen 2 gives you

Camera, mic, speaker — all real, all usable today

DAT exposes Gen 2's 12MP ultra-wide camera (still + up to 720p/30fps live stream), the 5-mic array, and speaker playback to your phone app. That covers every feature's input and output needs: face match, scam-call audio alerts, voice scheduling, SOS countdown.

What Gen 2 does not have

No display, no gesture wristband

The in-lens display and Neural Band EMG wristband are exclusive to the separate Ray-Ban Display model. Don't design toward "show a card of facts" for this hardware — there's nowhere to show it. Everything is spoken.

Bonus data point for facial recognition specifically: the dormant "Name Tag" facial-recognition code that EFF/Wired found already shipped inside Meta's own app (then pulled in June 2026) was found specifically on Ray-Ban Meta Gen 2 devices — the exact hardware you have. That's a direct confirmation the camera→face-match pipeline works end-to-end on this model; Meta already built and shipped it, just kept it switched off.
New UX wrinkle worth designing around: Gen 2's speakers are open-ear, not private in-ear audio — sound can leak and be faintly audible to whoever is standing close, especially somewhere quiet. Saying "this is your granddaughter Emma" out loud while looking directly at Emma is different from a silent display only the wearer sees: it can be overheard by the person being identified, which is either mildly awkward or socially useful ("caught not recognizing someone") depending on framing. Worth a deliberate script choice — e.g. keep phrasing generic/discreet, or lean into it as a feature ("glasses that help you greet people warmly") rather than hiding it.
The one real audio restriction: mic and hi-fi output are mutually exclusive. Per Meta's own DAT docs: "activating HFP switches the glasses away from A2DP, and audio output quality drops to 8 kHz mono for the duration of the session." Speaker-only playback (mic off) gets 44.1/48 kHz stereo; the moment the mic is listening — which is most of the time, since every feature here is voice-triggered — output drops to phone-call-quality mono. Design for that sound quality as the default, not the exception. Also: only one third-party app session can hold the glasses at a time, and (confirmed by a Meta maintainer on the DAT forum) there is no way to hook into or extend Meta AI's own voice pipeline — see the Voice commands section below.

Voice commands: fully buildable, zero integration with "Hey Meta"

Yes, you can build real voice commands — your own wake phrase, your own NLU, your own logic, running on DAT's streamed mic audio. What you cannot do is plug into, extend, or hand off with Meta's own assistant. These will run as two completely separate systems on the same glasses.

Straight from Meta's own DAT maintainer

Can we customize the "Hey Meta" wake word for our app?
"It is not possible to customise the 'Hey Meta' wake up word." — Oscar Falmer, Meta maintainer
developer forum, hands-on report:
"Meta AI does not have any API's, so no custom phrases, actions, handoff etc... nothing."

What you build instead

Your own always-listening keyword spotter running on the phone, fed by DAT's continuous mic stream, triggering your own NLU/logic, speaking back through the glasses. Fully within your control — just entirely separate from Meta AI. One anecdote in the DAT forum (an app called "oorion" reportedly responds to its own wake word on these glasses) is unconfirmed as an official partnership, but is consistent with — and suggestive evidence for — exactly this DIY approach working in production.

The only quasi-bridge that exists

One workaround surfaced in the forum: tell native Meta AI ("Hey Meta, message …") to relay a text message to an external bot, which then triggers your third-party logic. That's a delayed, text-relay hack dependent on Meta AI's own messaging integrations — not real-time voice command handoff. Don't design around it as a primary path.

UX implication: the wearer has to remember and use a second, different wake phrase specific to your app — on top of "Hey Meta" they may already know. Real risk of confusion for elderly users. Consider whether some features need a wake word at all: scam alerts and SOS are system-initiated (they push to the wearer, not the other way around, so no trigger phrase is needed), and scheduling could be triggered by a phone-app button tap instead of speech. Reserve your custom wake word for the one place voice genuinely helps most — e.g. "nice to meet you" naturally cueing facial recognition, since it's a phrase people already say.

Making it "live": streaming works, battery is the real wall

Live camera + mic streaming is real and proven — Meta's own "Live AI" mode already does continuous camera+mic streaming with an ongoing AI conversation about what the wearer is looking at. DAT gives third parties the same raw ingredients. The limiting factor isn't the SDK — it's power.

If Meta's own, chip-to-cloud-optimized feature only lasts ~30 minutes of continuous streaming, a third-party DAT app doing the same continuous video streaming will not beat that. "Always-on and live all day" is not realistic on Gen 2. Design around it, don't fight it.

The architecture that actually survives a day

  • Ambient layer, always on: mic-only streaming for the keyword spotter — audio draws far less power than video.
  • Camera, episodic only: open a live video stream for a few seconds right after a trigger (e.g. the facial-recognition phrase), run the match, close it. Don't stream video continuously "just in case" — it's both a battery killer and the exact always-watching-camera framing that got Meta's own Name Tag in trouble.
  • Keep the phone app active: per the DAT changelog, camera streaming keeps flowing at the transport level when the phone app backgrounds, but video decoding stops — so real-time facial-rec processing likely needs the companion app in an active/foreground-ish state. Test this early; it has real UX implications (can't just work while the phone sits locked in a pocket).
  • Scam detection needs none of this — it's a phone-side background notification listener, not a glasses stream, so it can run all day at effectively no glasses-battery cost.

1 · 2  Scheduling, reminders & keyword-triggered flows — build these first

This is the closest thing to a supported use case DAT already targets: stream mic audio to the phone, run your own lightweight keyword spotter (since you can't hook "Hey Meta" — confirmed on Meta's DAT GitHub discussions, issue threads #85 and #96), parse intent with an on-phone or cloud LLM, write to the phone's calendar/alarm APIs, and speak the confirmation back through the glasses' speaker.

Nothing here is glasses-specific engineering — it's a normal voice-assistant mobile app with the glasses as a hands-free mic/speaker. This is also the feature that best matches Angela's original framing: replace basic phone functions (calendar, alarms) with something ambient and non-screen-based. It works for both the 60s–70s "lifestyle" segment and the 80s–90s "medical need" segment the team was debating, so it doesn't force the demographic decision.

3  Scam detection — real, but Android-only

The glasses contribute nothing to detecting a scam call/text — that's entirely a phone-OS-level capability. On Android, a companion app using NotificationListenerService can read incoming call/SMS/Messenger/WhatsApp content, run it through an LLM classifier, and push a spoken flag to the glasses. This is exactly the mechanism Meta's own companion app already uses to announce calls/messages on Ray-Ban glasses, so the pattern is proven.

On iOS, Apple sharply restricts third-party access to call and SMS content — there's no equivalent open path. Scope this to an Android phone for the demo and say so explicitly rather than promising cross-platform.

4  Fall detection + emergency SOS — weakest technical footing

Two separate problems stack here. First, DAT's accelerometer/IMU exposure is described in Meta's own materials as something that "will eventually open up" — not confirmed shipped as of this research, so verify against the live v0.6 changelog before committing a demo to it. Second, even where IMU data exists, a head-mounted sensor is a known-weaker signal for fall detection than a wrist or waist-worn one (glasses rotate and can detach independently of the wearer's center of mass) — this is a documented limitation in the smart-eyewear fall-detection literature, not a solved problem the way Apple Watch's fall detection is.

Third, nothing in DAT places phone calls. The "auto-call 911" part has to be ordinary phone telephony API code, triggered by whatever signal the glasses provide. If you build this, frame it honestly as an SOS trigger with a countdown (accel spike + orientation change → phone-side confirmation UI → simulated/real call), not as validated fall detection.

Facial recognition: technically trivial, Meta itself just retreated from it

The plan's own note — "fine to demo at hackathon, address ethics post-event if pursuing further" — is reasonable for a controlled, consent-based demo, but shouldn't be read as low-risk for anything beyond that. Meta's own, much better-resourced legal team built this exact feature and pulled it back under pressure.

Legal exposure

Illinois's Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA) remains the sharpest U.S. exposure for face-geometry capture without consent — 107+ new suits filed in 2025 alone, Clearview AI settled for $51.75M. A 2024 amendment capped per-scan damages, applied retroactively by a 2026 7th Circuit ruling, but the statute still clearly covers this category. No smart-glasses-specific suit has landed yet — you'd be early, not safe.

What's actually fine to demo: face-matching against a small, explicitly-enrolled set of photos the elderly user's own family/caregiver consented to add (e.g. "this is your daughter Angela") — framed and pitched on stage as exactly that, never as general stranger identification.

Before you build

  • Confirm the exact Ray-Ban model — resolved: Gen 2. Design every output as spoken audio; no display path exists on this hardware. See Audio/Display.
  • Pull the live DAT v0.6 changelog and confirm it exposes Gen 2's IMU/accelerometer specifically before committing to fall detection — that access was described as "eventually" rolling out, not confirmed shipped.
  • Use Meta's Mock Device Kit to start coding now, since you have only one borrowed physical pair.
  • Settle the 60s–70s vs. 80s–90s target-demographic debate first — it directly decides whether fall detection/SOS (skews 80s–90s) is worth the build risk at all.

Framing for judges

  • Lead with scheduling/reminders + scam alerts as the shipped-feeling core.
  • Present facial ID explicitly as "consented family enrollment," and name-drop that you know about Meta's own Name Tag retreat — shows real diligence, not naivety.
  • Present fall detection as a labeled prototype/SOS trigger, not a finished safety feature.
Round 2

The latest brainstormed list, scored against the same constraints

Nine new/expanded feature ideas came out of the next brainstorm. None of them change the platform facts above — still Gen 2, still audio-only, still DAT's camera/mic/speaker/calendar/telephony surface, still the ~30 min continuous-streaming battery wall. Scored against that, plus one new cross-cutting legal issue that this batch surfaces for the first time.

New cross-cutting risk: recording other people's conversations. Three of these ideas (ambient voice-to-calendar, in-person scam detection, "find lost things" via recording history) all imply listening to or recording conversations the wearer is having with other people, not just the wearer's own commands. 12 U.S. states require all-party consent to record a private conversation — including California, Illinois, Florida, and Massachusetts — and smart glasses that transcribe or log audio in the background are exactly the fact pattern privacy-law commentary is currently flagging as high-risk, even though federal law (18 U.S.C. §2511) only requires one-party consent. This is a materially different risk class from the facial-recognition BIPA issue already covered above — it's about audio, not biometrics, and it's about strangers/acquaintances mid-conversation, not enrolled family photos.

Auto-scheduling — this is a different product, not a glasses feature

Everything else on both lists is "glasses as hands-free mic/camera for a phone app." Auto-scheduling is "AI agent that calls a dentist's office, negotiates a time slot, and commits the elderly user to it." That needs outbound telephony or web-form automation against arbitrary third-party businesses — a meaningfully larger system, with its own failure modes (books the wrong slot, mishears a callback number, commits someone to something they didn't approve). Demo-safe version: the glasses/phone flow drafts a request ("Call Dr. Kim's office, ask for the earliest cleaning slot after the 10th") and hands it to the wearer or a caregiver to actually send — keeps the value, removes the agent-acting-autonomously risk.

"Find lost things" — replace continuous recall with an explicit "tag it" moment

The pitch ("locate meds placed in a cabinet without being asked to remember") requires the glasses to have already been recording when the item was put away — i.e., a rolling video history of the wearer's home, on by default. That's the same shape as Microsoft's "Recall" feature, which drew heavy privacy criticism for exactly this pattern, and it's a harder version of the problem Meta already retreated from with Name Tag. It also just doesn't fit the hardware: continuous camera streaming is a ~30-minute battery budget, not an all-day one.

A scoped-down version keeps most of the value: the wearer says a trigger phrase ("remember this") while looking at where they're placing something, the glasses take one photo, tag it with a timestamp, and store it locally. Later, "where did I put my glasses cleaner" surfaces that one tagged photo. Opt-in, episodic, cheap on battery, and the wearer controls exactly what gets logged instead of everything being logged by default.

Facial recognition's "secretive database" — good privacy design, doesn't change the legal picture

Keeping face embeddings on-device, out of any cloud model, and non-exportable even to the user is a genuinely good design — it removes the breach/secondary-use/data-sale risk that drives most biometric-privacy litigation, and it's a strong, honest answer to "what happens to grandma's face data." It does not remove BIPA-style exposure, though: those statutes attach at the point of collecting a biometric identifier without informed consent, regardless of where it's later stored or who can see it. The already-recommended scope — enrolled family/caregiver photos only, explicit consent at enrollment, never pitched as general stranger-ID — still applies. Frame the on-device store as the responsible-storage half of the answer, and the consented-enrollment as the responsible-collection half; you need both.

New feature ideas — other elder pain points that fit this hardware

Picked for the same reason "appointment-card scanning" works well: they reuse a pipeline you're already building (episodic camera snap → vision/OCR → speak result, or voice command → phone API → speak result) instead of asking for new hardware capability.

Reuses the appointment-card pipeline

"Read this to me"

Point at a pill bottle, mail, a menu, a bank statement, small print on a package — one photo, OCR or a vision LLM, read it aloud. Probably the single best-fit idea on either list: it's the same snap-and-speak pipeline as appointment cards, directly addresses low-vision/small-print pain (one of the most common complaints in this age group), and has almost no legal exposure since it's reading the wearer's own documents.

Frame as label-reading, not medical advice

Medication mix-up check

Photograph a pill or bottle, cross-check against a stored prescription list, flag "this looks like your evening blood-pressure pill, not the morning one." High value for a real pain point (medication errors are a leading cause of elder ER visits), but say out loud that it's reading labels/matching a list the wearer's caregiver entered, never diagnosing or recommending — keeps it out of medical-device liability territory.

Simpler and safer than full missed-call detection

Contact-aware caller announcement

"It's your grandson Michael calling" instead of just a ringtone — match the incoming number against phone contacts and speak the relationship, not just the name. Solves a very common real complaint (elderly users not recognizing a name or missing quiet-vibrate calls) without needing notification-content scraping the way scam detection does.

Sidesteps the shaky IMU fall-detection problem

One-phrase emergency contact

A dedicated spoken phrase ("call my daughter" / "I need help") that triggers a phone call, independent of any fall/accelerometer signal. Delivers most of the safety value of the SOS feature today, without betting the demo on IMU access that's unconfirmed in DAT v0.6 or on head-worn fall-detection science that's genuinely weak.

Addresses isolation, not just tasks

Companion check-ins

A scheduled, opt-in "how's your day going" prompt, with an easy voice path to call a family member. Loneliness/social isolation is one of the most-cited quality-of-life issues for elderly users, and everything on both feature lists is task-oriented (calendar, scams, meds) — this is the one idea that targets the emotional/social pain point directly, using nothing but the already-built voice pipeline.

Feasible, but check the audio-quality tradeoff first

Audio wayfinding for errands

Spoken turn-by-turn directions through the glasses speaker, driven by the phone's own GPS (no glasses sensor needed, so no IMU dependency). Useful for the "walk to the pharmacy" case. One thing to test early: per the HFP tradeoff already documented above, having the mic open (for "where am I") while speaking directions back drops audio to 8 kHz mono — fine for speech, but confirm it's still intelligible outdoors before committing to it as a headline demo feature.
